Above all, I am fascinated by intelligence in all its forms—I see it woven into almost everything that shapes our world. More than anything, I want to understand how intelligence actually works. Because I believe intelligence already exists in nature, I view my job not as inventing it from scratch, but as discovering and deciphering what is already there.

In my day-to-day research, I approach this by studying neural nets mechanistically, drawing parallels to how biological systems process information. I see mapping their internal mechanics as our best shot at making advanced AI genuinely safe and governable.

A few questions currently driving my work:

* When a model throws unexpected outputs, how do we trace them back to the exact internal circuits responsible? And what level of abstraction makes those explanations actually useful?
* How do we catch subtle failure modes—like what people call deception, reward gaming, or specification gaming—that standard benchmarks tend to miss?
* How can we tell if a safety intervention truly changed a model's internal logic, instead of just teaching it to hide unwanted behavior during tests?
* What kind of concrete evidence do auditors and regulators actually need to verify safety claims before a model gets deployed?
* How do we scale up AI capabilities without slowly ceding important human choices to automated systems?

Beyond my core focus on interpretability, AI safety, and governance, I am drawn to computational cognitive science, policy, and poetry, among many other aspects of life.

My work has been published across venues including NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, ACL, EMNLP, FAccT, and Science Robotics.
